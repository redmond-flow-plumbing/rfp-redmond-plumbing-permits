Redmond Plumbing Permits: What Needs One and How It Works

You're planning a bathroom remodel, moving a water heater, or repiping the house — and someone mentions permits. Suddenly a straightforward project feels bureaucratic. Here's the plain-English guide to Redmond plumbing permits: which jobs need one, who handles the paperwork, and what actually happens on inspection day.

Which Plumbing Jobs Need a Permit in Redmond

The rule of thumb: if you're altering the plumbing system, you probably need a permit. Water heater replacements and relocations, sewer line repairs and replacements, repiping (partial or whole-house), new fixture installations where none existed, gas line work of any kind, and bathroom or kitchen remodels that move drains or supplies — all permitted. Sewer work often needs an additional side-sewer permit because it touches the public right-of-way. The logic is safety and accountability: pressurized water, gas, and sewage are exactly the systems you don't want installed wrong behind closed walls. When in doubt, call the city's permit center before you start — a five-minute phone call beats a stop-work order.

What Doesn't Need a Permit

Not everything requires paperwork. Like-for-like fixture swaps — replacing a faucet with a new faucet in the same spot, swapping a toilet for a new toilet on the existing flange — generally don't need permits. Clearing a clogged drain, replacing a flapper or fill valve, and swapping a showerhead are maintenance, not alterations. The gray area is "like-for-like" water heater swaps: many jurisdictions still want a permit even for same-size replacements, because venting, seismic strapping, and expansion tank requirements get verified at inspection. Our advice: if the job involves gas, a new water heater, or anything behind a wall, assume permitted until the city says otherwise.

Homeowner vs. Contractor: Who Pulls the Permit

Legally, either can — but there's a right answer in most cases. When a licensed contractor pulls the permit, the contractor owns the inspection results and any corrections. That's what you want: accountability sits with the professional. If a contractor suggests you pull the permit as the homeowner "to save money," be cautious — some use this to dodge responsibility for failed inspections, and a few aren't licensed to pull permits at all. A legitimate shop pulls its own permits as a matter of routine; it's built into our process and our pricing, not a favor. The permit itself is inexpensive relative to the job — what you're really buying is the paper trail that protects your home's value.

How the Inspection Flow Actually Works

Here's the part nobody explains. After the permit is issued, the work happens in stages, and the inspector visits at specific points — most critically the "rough-in" inspection, before walls are closed up. Your contractor schedules it, the inspector checks pipe sizing, slope, strapping, venting, and materials against code, and either signs off or lists corrections. Then walls close, fixtures set, and a final inspection confirms everything works. Failed inspections aren't disasters; they're usually minor corrections. But unpermitted work discovered later — during a sale, a remodel, or an insurance claim — is a real problem: buyers walk away, insurers deny claims, and the city can require you to open walls to prove what's inside. The permit costs a little now and saves a lot later.

Permits Protect Your Home's Resale Value

Here's the angle most homeowners miss: permits aren't just about the city — they're about your future buyer. When you sell, Washington's disclosure forms ask about unpermitted work, and buyers' inspectors flag anything that looks non-original. A permitted water heater replacement with a signed inspection card is a non-issue; an unpermitted one becomes a negotiation weapon that costs you far more than the permit ever would. We've seen deals stall over a bathroom remodel done without permits fifteen years earlier. Keep your permit cards and inspection sign-offs with your house records forever — they're proof your home's systems were installed right, and they're worth real money at closing.
